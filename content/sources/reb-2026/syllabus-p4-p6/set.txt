1.INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background to syllabus adaptation

The adaptation of the Science and Elementary Technology (SET) syllabus for upper primary reflects Rwanda's
ongoing commitment to strengthening the Competence-Based Curriculum (CBC), first rolled out in 2015. Continuous
feedback from teachers, school leaders, and education partners highlighted the need for improvements in content
delivery, time allocation, and responsiveness to learner needs.

This second edition incorporates the Ministerial directive to harmonize school hours to five per day and 33 weeks
of learning annually and ensures greater coherence, inclusiveness, and alignment with national priorities such as
Vision 2050 and the National Strategy for Transformation (NST2), which emphasize a knowledge-based economy,
responsible citizenship, and sustainable development.

2. TEACHING AND LEARNING SCIENCE AND ELEMENTARY TECHNOLOGY

2.1.The rationale of teaching and learning SET

    2.1.1.Science and Elementary Technology and society

Teaching Science and Elementary Technology to young children is critical for establishing a foundation for further
success in science and for coping with the demands of the 21st century. Furthermore, technology education
constitutes an unequalled important added value. Not only in developed countries but also in developing countries
such as Rwanda, the love and interest in science and technology begins in primary school where young children
tend to be more curious and motivated to learn.

The inclusion of Science and Elementary Technology in the Primary School reflects the importance of science and
technology in many aspects of our daily lives, at work, at school and at home. As integrated science, it provides a

SCIENCE AND ELEMENTARY TECHNOLOGY SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6  1
very good foundation for the study of science subjects in the post-primary setting. Most importantly, it cultivates
a positive attitude towards science and provides pupils with opportunities to experience the excitement of working
as a scientist.
Above all, the rationale of teaching and learning of SET is embedded in the need for pupils to have a greater awareness of the
role of science and technology in everyday life. SET at primary school enables the pupil to develop competencies which have
great impact on the society in general.

    2.1.1.Science and Elementary Technology and pupil

Pupils have to be prepared from an early age for active and responsible citizenship. With this regard, SET strives to
equip pupils to understand and situate scientific and technological developments in their cultural, environmental,
economic, political and social contexts. At the center of teaching and learning of SET, hands on activities will
play a key role, which in turn, should contribute significantly towards improving pupils' achievement, motivation,
technological literacy and test scores.

2.2.Competences

A competence is the ability to use an appropriate combination of knowledge, skills, attitudes, values, and behaviours
to accomplish a task successfully. In this syllabus, competences reflect a holistic blend of these elements, enabling
learners to apply them confidently and appropriately in diverse real-life situations.

By acquiring these competences, learners become better equipped to handle challenges, make informed decisions,
act responsibly, and contribute positively as active members of society. The competences are embedded in the
broad subject competences, yearly objectives, and unit-specific learning comptencies. Both generic competences
and basic competences must be emphasized and consistently reflected in the teaching and learning process.

Teachers are encouraged to design learning experiences that engage learners in meaningful tasks, allowing them
to practice and demonstrate competences both during and at the end of the learning process.

3. Pedagogical approach

The pedagogical approach in Science and Elementary Technology under the CBC emphasizes learner-centered,
participatory, and inclusive methods that develop knowledge, skills, values, and competences for responsible
citizenship. Teaching is interactive and active, engaging learners through debates, discussions, role-plays,
storytelling, field visits, projects, research, case studies, and reflective activities. These approaches ensure that
learning is enjoyable, personalized, and connected to real-life situations, while also fostering critical thinking,
moral reasoning, and problem-solving.

3.1.Role of the teacher

Teachers act as facilitators and guides, shifting away from traditional rote instruction. They organize learners
individually, in pairs, or in groups, and design purposeful tasks that promote inquiry, collaboration, and autonomy.
Teachers apply pedagogical principles of progression--moving from simple to complex, concrete to abstract, and
from learners' experiences to broader knowledge rooted in social, moral, and religious contexts. They integrate
cross-cutting issues such as peace, gender equality, environmental conservation, health, and cultural values,
while ensuring sensitivity to learners' diverse backgrounds. In Religious Education, teachers adopt an inclusive
approach by presenting different faiths objectively, encouraging respect, understanding of diverse beliefs, and
appreciation of moral and spiritual values.

3.2. Role of the pupil

Learners are active participants and co-creators of knowledge. Simply, it emphasizes the need for children to
think about scientific activity in order to make sense of and understand the scientific concepts being introduced.

8  SCIENCE AND ELEMENTARY TECHNOLOGY SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6
Specifically, when engaging in inquiry, pupils will describe objects and events, ask questions, construct explanations,
test those explanations against current scientific knowledge, and communicate their ideas to others. In SET
lesson, pupils get involved in the following activities (IAP, 2010, p. 9):

   · observing and, where possible, handling and manipulating real objects;
   · pursuing questions which they have identified as their own even if introduced by the teacher;
   · taking part in planning investigations with appropriate controls to answer specific questions;
   · using and developing skills of gathering data directly by observation or measurement and by using secondary

       sources;
   · using and developing skills of organizing and interpreting data, reasoning, proposing explanations, making

       predictions based on what they think or find out;
   · working collaboratively with others, communicating their own ideas and considering others' ideas;
   · expressing themselves using appropriate scientific terms and representations in writing and talk;
   · engaging in lively public discussions in defense of their work and explanations;
   · applying their learning in real-life contexts;
   · reflecting self-critically about the processes and outcomes of their inquiries.

During this reciprocal interaction, what pupils will acquire is not only content knowledge, but a number of skills
including how to approach a problem, identify important resources, design and carry out hands-on investigations,
analyze and interpret data, and, perhaps most importantly, recognize when they have answered the question or
solved the problem (Marsha, 2000).

4. Assessment approach

Assessment within the CBC is a fundamental component of the teaching and learning process. It is designed
to evaluate learners' progress across knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values, while simultaneously supporting
continuous learning and development. Unlike traditional models, CBC assessment emphasizes real-life problem-
solving, requiring learners to apply acquired competences to complex, everyday situations.

Assessment entails the systematic collection and interpretation of evidence to measure learner performance
against established standards. Beyond measuring achievement, it informs instructional improvement and
provides constructive feedback to learners, parents, and educators, thereby fostering accountability and guiding
educational decision-making.

4.1.Levels of assessment across terms and national examinations

To provide a comprehensive picture of learner achievement and development, assessment is conducted at multiple
levels:

      i. Diagnostic: Conducted before instruction to identify learners' prior knowledge and skills; administered at
          the beginning of a unit or lesson to guide teaching.

      ii. Formative / Continuous: Carried out during the learning process to track progress and provide feedback.
          They are implemented continuously through quizzes, projects, class activities, and teacher observations.

      iii. Summative assessment:

10  SCIENCE AND ELEMENTARY TECHNOLOGY SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6
   · End Term I: Administered after instruction to evaluate learners' achievement of Term I content. They are
       conducted at the end of the first term as a school-based assessment.

   · End Term II: District-coordinated assessment designed to measure achievement of Term II content. They
       developed by schools and quality-checked at district level.

   · End Term III: National assessment to evaluate mastery of the entire year's content. They are prepared, set,
       and moderated by NESA at the end of the third term.

National Examinations (NESA): High-stakes assessments for certification, selection, and accountability. They are
administered nationally at the end of key education cycles (P6, S3, S6) under NESA's authority. This examination
integrates objective questions, structured responses, and applied tasks aligned with CBC principles to holistically
measure learner achievement.

Average scores from school, district, and comprehensive summative assessments will be weighted and included
in the final national examination grading, initially contributing 10%, with the percentage gradually increasing as
teachers gain experience and confidence in assessment practices.

4.2. Record Keeping

Accurate record keeping is essential for monitoring learner progress and informing instructional decisions.
Teachers use tools such as portfolios, checklists, and report books to collect evidence of learning. Portfolios include
samples of learners' work, test results, projects, and self-reflections, serving as both a record of achievement and
a diagnostic tool for guiding remedial action. These records provide data for feedback to learners, teachers, and
parents, and contribute to final evaluations.

4.3.Item writing in summative assessment

Summative assessment items must be valid, fair, reliable, and aligned with CBC principles. They should test a

SCIENCE AND ELEMENTARY TECHNOLOGY SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6  11
range of competencies, including knowledge recall, application, analysis, and values integration, while reflecting
real-life contexts. Item writers follow a structured process that involves:

   · Identify topic areas to be tested from the subject syllabus.
   · Outline subject matter content to be considered as the basis for the test.
   · Identify learning outcomes to be measured by the test.
   · Preparing a test blueprint or table of specifications;
   · Using verbs that require higher-order thinking beyond memorization;
   · Applying transparent and consistent marking schemes.
   · Greater weight is given to higher-order cognitive skills as stated in the syllabus.

4.4. Structure and format of national examinations

At the completion of primary education, learners undertake a national examination designed to evaluate literacy,
numeracy, and competences in core subjects. For Science and Elementary Technology, a single combined paper is
administered, integrating both subject areas. The examination employs a variety of item types--such as objective
questions, structured responses, and applied tasks--intended to holistically assess both subject knowledge and
broader CBC competencies.

4.5.Reporting to parents

Regular communication with parents is an essential component of the CBC. This is achieved through progress
reports, parent­teacher meetings, and school community activities. Since the CBC emphasizes a wide range of
learning outcomes: knowledge, skills, attitudes, and competences--a single mark is insufficient to capture learner
progress. Effective reporting should highlight areas where learners are excelling as well as those requiring

6. SYLLABUS UNITS

6.1.Presentation of the structure of the syllabus units

The Science and Elementary Technology syllabus is designed as a single, integrated syllabus implemented uniformly
across all grades. Its structure follows a logical progression that ensures clarity, consistency, and alignment with
the Competence-Based Curriculum approach.

At each grade level, the syllabus is organized into Topic areas and each topic area is composed of Units, and
every unit is characterized by the following features:

   1. Alignment with lessons: Each unit corresponds to a specified number of lessons, ensuring effective time
       allocation and instructional planning.

   2. Key unit competence: Every unit is built around a Key unit competence that guides all teaching and Learning
       activities. This competence represents the overall learning goal to be achieved by both teacher and learners.

   3. Learning objectives: Each key unit competence is translated into specific Learning objectives, categorized
       as follows:

        · Type I (Knowledge and Understanding): Focused on foundational knowledge and comprehension, often
            referred to as Lower Order Thinking Skills (LOTS).

        · Type II and Type III (Skills, Attitudes, and Values): Aimed at developing practical skills, critical
            thinking, ethical values, and positive attitudes. These objectives, known as Higher Order Thinking Skills
            (HOTS), form the core focus of the adapted competence-based curriculum.

   4. Content specification: Each unit outlines the scope of content to be taught and learned, ensuring that
       teachers and learners work within the boundaries set by the stated Learning objectives.
