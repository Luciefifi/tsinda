1. GENERAL INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background to the syllabus adaptation

The adaptation of the Social Studies and Religious Education (SSRE ) syllabus for Upper Primary reflects Rwanda's
ongoing commitment to strengthening the Competence-Based Curriculum (CBC), first rolled out in 2015. Continuous
feedback from teachers, school leaders, and education partners highlighted the need for improvements in content
delivery, time allocation, and responsiveness to learner needs.

This second edition incorporates the Ministerial directive to harmonize school hours to five per day and 33 weeks
of learning annually and ensures greater coherence, inclusiveness, and alignment with national priorities such as
Vision 2050 and the National Strategy for Transformation (NST2), which emphasize a knowledge-based economy,
responsible citizenship, and sustainable development.

SSRE integrates social, cultural, civic, and spiritual dimensions of learning. It explores the learner's environment,
human relationships, ethical values, and religious understanding, while reflecting Rwanda's cultural diversity
and the evolving global context. Through this subject, pupils acquire knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values
that prepare them to address real-life challenges, make sound judgments, and grow into responsible, productive
citizens.

2.1. Rationale of Teaching and Learning Social and Religious Studies

Social Studies and Religious Education (SSRE ) is essential in nurturing well-rounded learners who understand their
society, cultural diversity, and spiritual values. It promotes ethical conduct, respect for others, and harmonious
coexistence, thereby strengthening unity and peace.

By studying SSRE, learners develop the competences needed to make informed decisions, appreciate moral and
spiritual beliefs, and contribute to social cohesion. The subject also empowers learners to play an active role in
national development by fostering responsibility, tolerance, and positive attitudes that align with Rwanda's vision
of building a peaceful and inclusive society.

i. Social Studies and Religious Education and Society

Social Studies and Religious Education (SSRE ) equips learners with knowledge of Rwanda's history, culture,
societal norms, civic responsibilities, and diverse religious traditions. It fosters understanding, tolerance, and
respect for diversity, which are essential for peaceful coexistence, social cohesion, and the strengthening of
national identity.

ii. Social Studies and Religious Education and Pupil

For pupils, SSRE develops critical thinking, empathy, and moral reasoning. It encourages self-reflection, builds
confidence, and shapes ethical behaviour. Through this subject, learners strengthen their identity, values, and
sense of responsibility, preparing them for lifelong learning and meaningful participation in society.

2.2. Competences

A competence is the ability to use an appropriate combination of knowledge, skills, attitudes, values, and behaviours
to accomplish a task successfully. In this syllabus, competences reflect a holistic blend of these elements, enabling
learners to apply them confidently and appropriately in diverse real-life situations.

By acquiring these competences, learners become better equipped to handle challenges, make informed decisions,
act responsibly, and contribute positively as active members of society. The competences are embedded in the
broad subject competences, yearly objectives, and unit-specific learning comptencies. Both generic competences
and basic competences must be emphasized and consistently reflected in the teaching and learning process.

Teachers are encouraged to design learning experiences that engage learners in meaningful tasks, allowing them
to practice and demonstrate competences both during and at the end of the learning process.

i. Generic competences

   · · Critical and problem-solving skills: Learners develop the ability to think broadly and objectively, evaluate
       their causes and consequences, and suggest peaceful and practical solutions that contribute to harmony and
       sustainable living in their communities.

       imagination beyond the knowledge provided, to generate new ideas and construct new concepts.

   · Research: This will help learners acquire the ability to gather, analyze, and interpret information from a
       variety of sources, including community elders, religious texts, and cultural traditions. This competence
       enables them to solve problems related to morality, cultural values, technologies, and everyday behaviors
       based on informed and ethical principles.

   · Communication in official languages: Teachers, irrespective of not being teachers of language, will ensure
       the proper use of the language of instruction. This will help learners develop the capacity to communicate
       clearly, confidently, and respectfully in both spoken and written forms. By using appropriate vocabulary

2.3 Values

Values are enduring beliefs, principles, and dispositions that influence how individuals think, feel, make decisions,
interact with others, and apply knowledge, skills and attitudes in different situations. Within the Competence-Based
Curriculum, values provide the ethical, social and cultural foundation for learning and responsible citizenship. The
Rwanda Curriculum Framework recognizes two complementary categories of values: Curriculum Values, which
guide the philosophy, design and implementation of education and Basic Values, which represent the moral, civic,
and character attributes that learners are expected to internalize and demonstrate in their daily lives. Together,
these values shape the development of competent, responsible, self-reliant, and socially engaged citizens.

    2.3.1 Curriculum values

   · Excellence: Striving for the highest standards in learning, behaviour, and performance by consistently
       producing quality work, using feedback for improvement and taking pride in achievement. It is reflected in
       accuracy, continuous growth and encouraging excellence in others (Pupils practise movements repeatedly
       to improve their running, jumping, balancing, throwing and catching skills. They celebrate personal
       improvement rather than winning only).

   · Aspiration and optimism: Believing in one's potential and pursuing ambitious goals with confidence,
       perseverance, and a positive outlook. It involves viewing challenges as opportunities for growth and inspiring
       hope in oneself and others (Pupils are encouraged to try new physical activities with confidence, complete
       simple movement challenges, and believe they can improve through practice.)

   · Equity and inclusiveness: Valuing the dignity and worth of every individual by promoting fairness, equal
       participation, and opportunities for all. It is demonstrated through respect for diversity, support for others,
       and opposition to discrimination and exclusion (Every pupil participates in games regardless of gender,
       ability or background. Teachers adapt activities so that all children can play safely and successfully).

3. PEDAGOGICAL APPROACH

The pedagogical approach in Social Studies and Religious Education under the CBC emphasizes learner-centered,
participatory, and inclusive methods that develop knowledge, skills, values, and competences for responsible
citizenship. Teaching is interactive and active, engaging learners through debates, discussions, role-plays,
storytelling, field visits, projects, research, case studies, and reflective activities. These approaches ensure that
learning is enjoyable, personalized, and connected to real-life situations, while also fostering critical thinking,
moral reasoning, and problem-solving.

      a. Role of the Teacher

Teachers act as facilitators and guides, shifting away from traditional rote instruction. They organize learners
individually, in pairs, or in groups, and design purposeful tasks that promote inquiry, collaboration, and autonomy.
Teachers apply pedagogical principles of progression--moving from simple to complex, concrete to abstract, and
from learners' experiences to broader knowledge rooted in social, moral, and religious contexts. They integrate
cross-cutting issues such as peace, gender equality, environmental conservation, health, and cultural values,
while ensuring sensitivity to learners' diverse backgrounds. In Religious Education, teachers adopt an inclusive

approach by presenting different faiths objectively, encouraging respect, understanding of diverse beliefs, and
appreciation of moral and spiritual values.

      b. Role of the Learner

Learners are active participants and co-creators of knowledge. They engage in inquiry, debates, problem-solving,
and group collaboration while drawing connections between personal experiences, social realities, and religious
teachings. Through these activities, learners develop critical thinking, communication, respect for diversity, moral
responsibility, and the ability to apply knowledge in everyday life and societal challenges.

      c. Inclusive and Special Needs Education

In line with Rwanda's inclusive education policy, all learners regardless of ability have the right to access quality
education. Teachers adapt teaching methods, materials, and assessment strategies to meet diverse needs,
including those of learners with disabilities. Differentiated instruction, assistive technologies, peer support, and
individualized approaches are used to ensure equitable participation and achievement of learning outcomes. This
inclusive approach recognizes learners' varied paces, experiences, and abilities, creating an environment where
every learner can thrive.

4. ASSESSMENT APPROACH

Assessment within the CBC is a fundamental component of the teaching and learning process. It is designed
to evaluate learners' progress across knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values, while simultaneously supporting
continuous learning and development. Unlike traditional models, CBC assessment emphasizes real-life problem-
solving, requiring learners to apply acquired competences to complex, everyday situations.

Assessment entails the systematic collection and interpretation of evidence to measure learner performance
against established standards. Beyond measuring achievement, it informs instructional improvement and
provides constructive feedback to learners, parents, and educators, thereby fostering accountability and guiding
educational decision-making.

          a. Levels of assessment across terms and national examinations
To provide a comprehensive picture of learner achievement and development, assessment is conducted at multiple
levels:

     (i) Diagnostic: Conducted before instruction to identify learners' prior knowledge and skills;
          administered at the beginning of a unit or lesson to guide teaching.

     (ii) Formative / Continuous: Carried out during the learning process to track progress and provide
          feedback; implemented continuously through quizzes, projects, class activities, and teacher
          observations.

     (iii) Summative assessment:

       · End Term I: Administered after instruction to evaluate learners' achievement of Term I content;
           conducted at the end of the first term as a school-based assessment.

       · End Term II: District-coordinated assessment designed to measure achievement of Term II content;
           developed by schools and quality-checked at district level.

       · End Term III: National assessment to evaluate mastery of the entire year's content; prepared, set, and
           moderated by NESA at the end of the third term.

       · National Examinations (NESA): High-stakes assessments for certification, selection, and accountability;
           administered nationally at the end of key education cycles (P6, S3, S6) under NESA's authority. This
           examination integrates objective questions, structured responses, and applied tasks aligned with CBC
           principles to holistically measure learner achievement.

Average scores from school, district, and comprehensive summative assessments will be weighted and included
in the final national examination grade, initially contributing 10%, with the percentage gradually increasing as
teachers gain experience and confidence in assessment practices.

           b. Record keeping

Accurate record keeping is essential for monitoring learner progress and informing instructional decisions.
Teachers use tools such as portfolios, checklists, and report books to collect evidence of learning. Portfolios include
samples of learners' work, test results, projects, and self-reflections, serving as both a record of achievement and
a diagnostic tool for guiding remedial action. These records provide data for feedback to learners, teachers, and
parents, and contribute to final evaluations.

           c. Item writing in summative assessment

Summative assessment items must be valid, fair, reliable, and aligned with CBC principles. They should test a
range of competencies, including knowledge recall, application, analysis, and values integration, while reflecting
real-life contexts. Item writers follow a structured process that involves:

   · Identify topic areas to be tested from the subject syllabus.

   · Outline subject matter content to be considered as the basis for the test.

   · Identify learning outcomes to be measured by the test.

   · Preparing a test blueprint or table of specifications;

   · Using verbs that require higher-order thinking beyond memorization;

   · Applying transparent and consistent marking schemes.

Greater weight is given to higher-order cognitive skills as stated in the syllabus.

           d. Structure and format of national examinations

At the completion of primary education, learners undertake a national examination designed to evaluate literacy,
numeracy, and competences in core subjects. For Social Studies and Religious Education, a single combined paper

questions, structured responses, and applied tasks--intended to holistically assess both subject knowledge and
broader CBC competencies.

           e. Reporting to parents

Regular communication with parents is an essential component of the CBC. This is achieved through progress
reports, parent­teacher meetings, and school community activities. Since the CBC emphasizes a wide range of
learning outcomes: knowledge, skills, attitudes, and competences--a single mark is insufficient to capture learner
progress. Effective reporting should highlight areas where learners are excelling as well as those requiring
improvement. Using descriptive feedback or simple rating scales (e.g., meeting expectations very well, meeting
expectations, not meeting expectations) across knowledge, subject-specific skills, and competences provides a
clearer and more comprehensive picture than numerical grades alone. This approach fosters meaningful parental
involvement and supports learners' continued growth.

6. SYLLABUS UNITS

           a. Presentation of the structure of the syllabus

The Social Studies and Religious Education syllabus is designed as a single, integrated syllabus implemented
uniformly across all grades. Its structure follows a logical progression that ensures clarity, consistency, and
alignment with the Competence-Based Curriculum approach.

At each grade level, the syllabus is organized into Topic Areas, which are further broken down into Sub-Topic
Areas. Each Sub-Topic Area is composed of Units, and every unit is characterized by the following features:

    1. Alignment with Lessons: Each unit corresponds to a specified number of lessons, ensuring
         effective time allocation and instructional planning.

    2. Key Unit Competence: Every unit is built around a Key Unit Competence that guides all teaching
         and learning activities. This competence represents the overall learning goal to be achieved by
         both teacher and learners.

    3. Learning Objectives: Each key unit competence is translated into specific learning objectives,
         categorized as follows:

       · Type I (Knowledge and Understanding): Focused on foundational knowledge and comprehension, often
           referred to as Lower Order Thinking Skills (LOTS).

       · Type II and Type III (Skills, Attitudes, and Values): Aimed at developing practical skills, critical
           thinking, ethical values, and positive attitudes. These objectives, known as Higher Order Thinking Skills
           (HOTS), form the core focus of the adapted Competence-Based Curriculum.

    4. Content Specification: Each unit outlines the scope of content to be taught and learned,
         ensuring that teachers and learners work within the boundaries set by the stated learning
         objectives.

Generic competencies:

· Critical Thinking and Problem-Solving Skills: Learners develop the ability to analyse socio-economic

   economic challenges.

· Research Skills: Learners develop research skills by collecting information about their district, its location,
   population activities, and economic practices through maps, observation, interviews with community members,
   and other sources of information. They learn how to organize, analyse, and present collected information.

   resources. They learn to respect themselves, others, and the contributions of different people in their district.

· Self-reliance: Learners develop self-reliance by understanding the importance of using available resources
   and skills to improve their lives and contribute to the development of their district. They learn that individuals
   and communities can create opportunities through productive activities.

· National and Cultural Identity: Learners strengthen their national and cultural identity by learning about
   the socio-economic activities practiced in their district and other parts of Rwanda. They appreciate local
   traditions, occupations, and ways of life that contribute to Rwanda's development.

   environmental protection. They discuss how activities such as agriculture, trade, industries, and settlement

   waste management, and responsible use of natural resources to support long-term development.

Assessment criteria: A learner is able to exhibit self-control and discernment in avoiding and managing
situations that may lead to conflicts.

Generic competencies:

   · Critical Thinking and Problem-Solving Skills: This will be developed by helping learners identify causes
       and effects of different conflicts, analyze conflict situations, and suggest peaceful solutions through
       dialogue, negotiation, and cooperation.

   · Cooperation, Interpersonal Management, and Life Skills: This will be developed through group
       discussions, role plays, and activities that help learners practice empathy, active listening, teamwork,
       respect for others, and peaceful ways of managing disagreements.

   · Communication: This will be developed by engaging learners in discussions, debates, and presentations
       where they express ideas clearly, listen to others' opinions, and use respectful language during conflict
       resolution.

   · Research Skills: This will be developed by allowing learners to explore examples of conflicts from their
       communities and other societies, collect information, and identify effective methods used to resolve them.

Basic values:

   · Peace and Tolerance: This will be developed by helping learners understand the importance of dialogue,
       forgiveness, respect for differences, and peaceful approaches to solving disagreements.

   · Respect for Others and Human Rights: This will be developed by teaching learners to listen to others,
       value different opinions, respect people's rights, and avoid actions that cause harm or discrimination.

   · Justice: This will be developed by helping learners understand fairness, equal treatment, and the importance
       of finding balanced solutions when resolving conflicts.

   · Solidarity and Democracy: This will be developed through group activities where learners practice
       cooperation, shared decision-making, negotiation, and working together to find solutions.

Cross cutting issues:

   · Peace and Values Education: This will be addressed by helping learners understand causes of conflicts,
       develop peaceful ways of solving disagreements, and practice values such as tolerance, empathy,
       forgiveness, and respect.

   · Genocide Studies: This will be addressed by helping learners understand how hatred, discrimination, and
       intolerance can lead to serious conflicts and violence. Learners will develop commitment to preventing
       divisionism and promoting unity.

   · Inclusive Education: This will be addressed by encouraging learners to respect different opinions,
       backgrounds, and abilities. Learners will practice inclusion and peaceful interaction with all members of
       society.

   · Impact of Social Media: This will be addressed by helping learners understand how social media can either
       increase conflicts through misinformation and harmful messages or support peace through responsible
       communication.

Links to other Subjects: Social studies
