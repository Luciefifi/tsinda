1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background to syllabus adaptation

The adaptation of the English syllabus for lower primary reflects Rwanda's ongoing commitment to strengthening
the Competence-Based Curriculum (CBC), first rolled out in 2015. Continuous feedback from teachers, school
leaders, and education partners highlighted the need for improvements in content delivery, time allocation, and
responsiveness to learner needs.

This second edition incorporates the Ministerial directive to harmonize school hours to five per day and 33 weeks
of learning annually and ensures greater coherence, inclusiveness, and alignment with national priorities such as
Vision 2050 and the National Strategy for Transformation (NST2), which emphasize a knowledge-based economy,
responsible citizenship, and sustainable development.

Through this subject, pupils acquire knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values that prepare them to address real-life
challenges, make sound judgments, and grow into responsible, productive citizens.

2. TEACHING AND LEARNING ENGLISH

2.1. Rationale

English is a global language of education, information, trade, diplomacy, science, technology, the internet, and
travel. It is the international common tongue and the most widely learned foreign language in the world. In
Rwanda--an English-speaking nation, and a member of both the East African Community and the Commonwealth--
English serves as the language of instruction and a vital link to regional and global communities. Mastery of
English at the upper primary level is essential not only for enhancing learning across the curriculum but also for
preparing learners to communicate effectively in diverse national and international contexts

The current English curriculum is competence-based, focusing on developing the whole learner by equipping them
with strong oral, reading, and writing skills, alongside critical thinking, collaboration, and positive attitudes.
Learning activities are designed to move beyond mere language knowledge, enabling pupils to use English to
reason, express themselves, and solve problems in varied real-life situations. This approach ensures that learners
can compete favourably, participate fully in East Africa and the wider global community, and achieve higher levels
of learning achievement.

    2.1.1. English and society

Communication lies at the heart of every development trend in any country, and this is especially true for Rwanda.
Learning English will not only help pupils succeed in their examinations but will also equip them with skills that will
enable them, after school, to conduct business effe tively and seize opportunities both locally and internationally,
particularly in contexts where English proficiency is a key requirement

For this reason, English is taught as a subject from nursery through to university and is fully used as the language
of instruction from Primary level onwards. At lower primary level, pupils are expected to build on the solid language
foundation acquired in pre-primary education.

Before English was adopted as both an officia language and the language of instruction in Rwandan schools,
Rwandans faced challenges in engaging effectively, conducting business, and fully benefiting from opportunities
in East Africa, the Commonwealth, and the wider English-speaking world. The introduction of English in Rwanda
addresses these challenges and opens up broader social, academic, and economic prospects for learners.

    2.1.2. English and the Pupils

English is essential for pupils at this level as it enables them to progress and succeed in other subjects at higher
levels of education, since it serves as the medium of instruction from primary school onwards. As one of Rwanda's
officia languages, English also equips pupils with the skills needed to participate effectively in business and
professional settings within the country.

2.2. Competences

A competence is the ability to use an appropriate combination of knowledge, skills, attitudes, values, and behaviours
to accomplish a task successfully. In this syllabus, competences reflect a holistic blend of these elements, enabling
learners to apply them confidently and appropriately in diverse real-life situations

By acquiring these competences, learners become better equipped to handle challenges, make informed decisions,
act responsibly, and contribute positively as active members of society. The competences are embedded in the
broad subject competences, yearly objectives, and unit-specific learning comptencies. Both generic competences
and basic competences must be emphasized and consistently reflected in the teaching and learning process

Teachers are encouraged to design learning experiences that engage learners in meaningful tasks, allowing them
to practice and demonstrate competences both during and at the end of the learning process.

    2.2.1. Generic competences

   · Critical and problem-solving skills: Learners develop the ability to think broadly and objectively, evaluate
       their causes and consequences, and suggest peaceful and practical solutions that contribute to harmony and
       sustainable living in their communities.

   · Creativity and innovation: The acquisition of such skills will help learners to take the initiative and use
       imagination beyond the knowledge provided, to generate new ideas and construct new concepts.

2.3 Values

Values are enduring beliefs, principles, and dispositions that influence how individuals think, feel, make decisions,
interact with others, and apply knowledge, skills, and attitudes in different situations. Within the Competence-
Based Curriculum, values provide the ethical, social, and cultural foundation for learning and responsible
citizenship. The Rwanda Curriculum Framework recognizes two complementary categories of values:

­ Curriculum Values, which guide the philosophy, design, and implementation of education

­ Basic Values, which represent the moral, civic, and character attributes that learners are expected to internalize
   and demonstrate in their daily lives. Together, these values shape the development of competent, responsible,
   self-reliant, and socially engaged citizens.

opposition to discrimination and exclusion.

It is reflect d in clear communication, responsible decision-making, openness to feedback, and fairness in

3. PEDAGOGICAL APPROACH

The pedagogical approach in English under the CBC emphasizes learner-centered, participatory, and inclusive
methods that develop knowledge, skills, values, and competences for responsible citizenship. Teaching is interactive
and active, engaging learners through debates, discussions, role-plays, storytelling, field visits, projects, research,
case studies, and reflective activities. These approaches ensure that learning is enjoyable, personalized, and
connected to real-life situations, while also fostering critical thinking, moral reasoning, and problem-solving.

3.1. Role of the teacher

Teachers act as facilitators and guides, shifting away from traditional rote instruction. They organize learners
individually, in pairs, or in groups, and design purposeful tasks that promote inquiry, collaboration, and autonomy.
Teachers apply pedagogical principles of progression--moving from simple to complex, concrete to abstract, and
from learners' experiences to broader knowledge rooted in social, moral, and religious contexts. They integrate
cross-cutting issues such as peace, gender equality, environmental conservation, health, and cultural values,
while ensuring sensitivity to learners' diverse backgrounds. In Religious Education, teachers adopt an inclusive
approach by presenting different faiths objectively, encouraging respect, understanding of diverse beliefs, and
appreciation of moral and spiritual values.

3.2. Role of the Learner

Learners are active participants and co-creators of knowledge. They engage in inquiry, debates, problem-solving,
and group collaboration while drawing connections between personal experiences, social realities, and religious
teachings. Through these activities, learners develop critical thinking, communication, respect for diversity, moral
responsibility, and the ability to apply knowledge in everyday life and societal challenges.

3.3. Inclusive and Special Needs Education

In line with Rwanda's inclusive education policy, all learners regardless of ability have the right to access quality
education. Teachers adapt teaching methods, materials, and assessment strategies to meet diverse needs,
including those of learners with disabilities. Differ ntiated instruction, assistive technologies, peer support, and
individualized approaches are used to ensure equitable participation and achievement of learning outcomes. This
inclusive approach recognizes learners' varied paces, experiences, and abilities, creating an environment where
every learner can thrive.

4. Assessment Approach

Assessment within the CBC is a fundamental component of the teaching and learning process. It is designed
to evaluate learners' progress across knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values, while simultaneously supporting
continuous learning and development. Unlike traditional models, CBC assessment emphasizes real-life problem-
solving, requiring learners to apply acquired competences to complex, everyday situations.

Assessment entails the systematic collection and interpretation of evidence to measure learner performance
against established standards. Beyond measuring achievement, it informs instructional improvement and
provides constructive feedback to learners, parents, and educators, thereby fostering accountability and guiding
educational decision-making.

4.1. Levels of assessment across terms and national examinations

To provide a comprehensive picture of learner achievement and development, assessment is conducted at multiple
levels:

    i) Diagnostic: Conducted before instruction to identify learners' prior knowledge and skills; administered at
         the beginning of a unit or lesson to guide teaching.

10  ENGLISH LANGUAGE SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6  Experimental version
    ii) Formative / Continuous: Carried out during the learning process to track progress and provide feedback;
         implemented continuously through quizzes, projects, class activities, and teacher observations.

    iii) Summative assessment:

         · End Term I: Administered after instruction to evaluate learners' achievement of Term I content;
             conducted at the end of the first term as a school-based assessment

         · End Term II: District-coordinated assessment designed to measure achievement of Term II content;
             developed by schools and quality-checked at district level.

         · End Term III: National assessment to evaluate mastery of the entire year's content; prepared, set, and
             moderated by NESA at the end of the third term.

         · National Examinations (NESA): High-stakes assessments for certification,selection, and accountability;
             administered nationally at the end of key education cycles (P6, S3, S6) under NESA's authority. This
             examination integrates objective questions, structured responses, and applied tasks aligned with CBC
             principles to holistically measure learner achievement.

Average scores from school, district, and comprehensive summative assessments will be weighted and included
in the final national examination grading, initially contributing 10%, with the percentage gradually increasing as
teachers gain experience and confidence in assessment practices

4.2. Record keeping

Accurate record keeping is essential for monitoring learner progress and informing instructional decisions.
Teachers use tools such as portfolios, checklists, and report books to collect evidence of learning. Portfolios include
samples of learners' work, test results, projects, and self-reflections, serving as both a record of achievement and
a diagnostic tool for guiding remedial action. These records provide data for feedback to learners, teachers, and
parents, and contribute to final evaluations

4.3. Item writing in summative assessment

Summative assessment items must be valid, fair, reliable, and aligned with CBC principles. They should test a
range of competencies, including knowledge recall, application, analysis, and values integration, while reflecting
real-life contexts. Item writers follow a structured process that involves:

   · Identify topic areas to be tested from the subject syllabus.
   · Outline subject matter content to be considered as the basis for the test.
   · Identify learning outcomes to be measured by the test.
   · Preparing a test blueprint or table of specifications
   · Using verbs that require higher-order thinking beyond memorization;
   · Applying transparent and consistent marking schemes.
   · Greater weight is given to higher-order cognitive skills as stated in the syllabus.

the written national examination, and three additional sections that are optional for the national examination
but strongly recommended for School-level assessment. These sections are designed to test pupils' mastery of all

4.5. Reporting to parents

Regular communication with parents is an essential component of the CBC. This is achieved through progress
reports, parent­teacher meetings, and school community activities. Since the CBC emphasizes a wide range of
learning outcomes: knowledge, skills, attitudes, and competences--a single mark is insufficien to capture learner
progress. Effective reporting should highlight areas where learners are excelling as well as those requiring
improvement. Using descriptive feedback or simple rating scales (e.g., meeting expectations very well, meeting
expectations, not meeting expectations) across knowledge, subject-specific skills, and competences provides a
clearer and more comprehensive picture than numerical grades alone. This approach fosters meaningful parental
involvement and supports learners' continued growth.

5. RESOURCES

The effective implementation of the English syllabus requires the provision and utilization of appropriate resources.
These resources include both teaching and learning materials and qualified human personnel, without which the
delivery of the syllabus would be ineffective

5.1. Materials needed for implementation

Teaching and learning of English are presented in context to give pupils meaningful opportunities to practice the
language. Effective implementation of this curriculum requires a variety of teaching and learning materials. These
include: textbooks, teachers' guides, audio-visual resources, charts, flashcards, real objects, maps, pictures,
storybooks, graded readers, and supplementary reading materials. ICT tools such as language learning software,
videos, and digital dictionaries can also enhance learning. To cover all language skills, teachers should use materials
that support listening (audio recordings, songs, dialogues), speaking (role-play materials, conversation cards),
reading (graded texts, storybooks), writing (workbooks, writing prompts), grammar and vocabulary (posters,

5.2. Human Resource

The successful implementation of this syllabus relies heavily on qualified, competent, and motivated human
resources. The following are key personnel required:

   · Teachers: Professional and well-trained teachers with subject expertise and strong pedagogical skills in
       learner-centered, participatory, and inclusive methods. Teachers should model integrity, tolerance, respect,
       and responsibility, and apply effective assessment strategies

   · Community resource persons: cultural leaders, and professionals from different sectors who can be invited
       as guest speakers, mentors, or facilitators to enrich classroom learning with practical, authentic perspectives
       and contextual insights.

   · Parents and guardians: Partners in education who provide moral support, monitor learner progress, and
       collaborate with teachers to reinforce values, attitudes, and competences at home, thereby ensuring holistic
       learner development.

Effective collaboration among these stakeholders fosters an enabling learning environment, equipping learners
not only with academic knowledge but also with values and competences required for responsible citizenship,
ethical conduct, and social harmony.

6. SYLLABUS UNITS

6.1. Presentation of the structure of the syllabus

The English syllabus is designed as a single, integrated syllabus implemented uniformly across all grades. Its
structure follows a logical progression that ensures clarity, consistency, and alignment with the Competence-
Based Curriculum approach.
At each grade level, the syllabus is organized into two main topic areas: oral communication and written
communication, which apply across all levels of education. English is taught through different contexts and
situations, which are presented as learning units.
Every unit is characterized by the following features:

   1. Alignment with Lessons: Each unit corresponds to a specified number of lessons, ensuring effecti e time
       allocation and instructional planning.

   2. Key Unit Competence: Every unit is built around a Key Unit Competence that guides all teaching and learning
       activities. This competence represents the overall learning goal to be achieved by both teacher and learners.

   3. Learning Objectives: Each key unit competence is translated into specific learning objectives, categorized
       as follows:

       · Type I (Knowledge and Understanding): Focused on foundational knowledge and comprehension, often
            referred to as Lower Order Thinking Skills (LOTS).

       · Type II and Type III (Skills, Attitudes, and Values): Aimed at developing practical skills, critical
            thinking, ethical values, and positive attitudes. These objectives, known as Higher Order Thinking Skills
            (HOTS), form the core focus of the adapted Competence-Based Curriculum.

   4. Content Specification Each unit outlines the scope of content to be taught and learned, ensuring that
       teachers and learners work within the boundaries set by the stated learning objectives.

    6.2.1. Key competences at the end of P4

By the end of P4, the Pupil should be able to:

Reading
   · Read and show understanding of short, simple texts of 50­80 words, identifying main ideas and some
       supporting details.
   · Recognise and read common sight words, and use context to guess meanings of simple unfamiliar words.

Writing
   · Write short guided sentences and simple paragraphs of 30­50 words for familiar purposes (e.g., describing
       self, family, daily activities).
   · Use basic punctuation (full stop, question mark, capital letters) and common linking words (e.g., and,
       because).

Listening

   · Listen to and show understanding of short-spoken texts of 30­50 words (stories, instructions, conversations)
       and identify main ideas.

   · Follow two-step oral instructions accurately.

Speaking

   · Speak in simple sentences of 5­8 words to describe, tell short stories, or give information about familiar
       topics.

   · Participate in short dialogues, asking and answering simple questions politely.

Generic Competences

The following generic competences are developed through the learning activities in this unit:

   · Communication in officia languages: Developed as learners discuss family relationships, give advice,
       practise reported speech, and participate in role-plays about family harmony.

   · Critical thinking and problem-solving: Developed as learners identify causes of family conflicts, analyse
       situations, and suggest peaceful solutions through discussions and writing activities.

Basic Values

The following basic values are promoted throughout this unit:

   · Peace and peaceful coexistence: Promoted as learners use English to discuss communication, forgiveness,
       understanding, and peaceful ways of resolving conflicts.

   · Respect for others and human rights: Promoted as learners discuss respecting family roles, rules,
       opinions, and differences among family members.

Cross-Cutting Issues

The following cross-cutting issues are integrated into the learning activities in this unit:

   · Peace and Values Education: Promoted as learners practise respectful communication, cooperation, and
       conflict resolution through dialogues and role-plays.

   · Gender Education: Promoted as learners discuss fair sharing of family roles and responsibilities among all
       family members.

Assessment criteria: Can describe ways to maintain harmony in the family, express feelings and resolve family
conflicts through role-play, read and understand stories about family harmony, write short messages or dialogues
showing respect and cooperation in the family, value harmony, respect, and peaceful communication.
