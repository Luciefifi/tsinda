1. GENERAL INTRODUCTION

The adaptation of the Mathematics syllabus for Upper Primary reflects Rwanda's ongoing commitment to
strengthening the Competence-Based Curriculum (CBC), first rolled out in 2015. Continuous feedback from
teachers, school leaders and education partners highlighted the need for improvements in content delivery, time
allocation and responsiveness to learner needs.

This second edition incorporates the Ministerial directive to harmonize school periods to five per day and 33
weeks of learning annually and ensures greater coherence, inclusiveness and alignment with national priorities
such as Vision 2050 and the National Strategy for Transformation (NST2), which emphasize a knowledge-based
economy, responsible citizenship and sustainable development.

The new Upper Primary Mathematics syllabus guides the interaction between the teacher and the learner through
the learning processes and highlights the essential practical skills and competences a learner should acquire
during and at the end of each unit of learning. Through this subject, pupils acquire knowledge, skills, attitudes,
and values that prepare them to address real-life challenges, make sound judgments, and grow into responsible,
productive citizens.

2. Teaching and learning Mathematics

2.1 Rationale of Teaching and Learning Mathematics

Teaching mathematics in upper primary (P4-P6) builds directly upon the foundation laid in lower primary but
shifts towards greater abstraction, formal application and preparation for future academic and life paths.

The primary rationale is to solidify Mathematical competence and transition students from concrete, hands-on
learning to more abstract reasoning and problem-solving. This involves mastering complex operations with whole
numbers, diving deeply into fractions, decimals, percentages and being introduced to the formal language of

viii  MATHEMATICS SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6
geometry. The goal is to ensure students achieve numerical fluency which is a prerequisite for secondary level
mathematics and practical daily tasks like financial planning and data interpretation.

Furthermore, upper primary mathematics is explicitly geared towards equipping students with the analytical
tools needed for Rwanda's socio-economic development. This includes a strong emphasis on mathematics, such as
data handling and likelihood of events, to create informed citizens who can critically evaluate data, assess risks,
and contribute to a data-driven society. The curriculum also intensifies its focus on developing problem-solving
skills, preparing students for the demands of secondary education and ultimately fostering the future innovators,
technicians, and critical thinkers central to achieving Rwanda's Vision 2050.

    2.1.1 Mathematics and Society

The upper Primary Mathematics syllabus emphasizes integrated skills and an integrated approach across all
disciplines. Mathematics is an excellent vehicle for developing and improving a person's intellectual competence in
logical reasoning, spatial visualization, analysis, and abstract thought. Learning mathematics fosters numeracy,
logical reasoning, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills. This results in the application of mathematics to
many daily activities, thereby making it an important tool for society.

Therefore, mathematics plays an important role in society through counting, calculation, measurement, and
the systematic study of shapes and motion. It is also essential in fields such as natural sciences, engineering,
medicine, finance, and the social sciences. Applied mathematics, including statistics and probability, plays a vital
role in games of chance, the national census process, scientific research, and more. Furthermore, cross-cutting
issues like financial literacy are incorporated into mathematics units to improve the social and economic welfare
of Rwandan society.

    2.1.2 Mathematics and Pupil

Pupils require fundamental Mathematical competences such as counting, measuring, financial management,
and data interpretation to function effectively as citizens in Rwanda. These skills are essential for daily tasks,

development.

Furthermore, mathematics provides the knowledge, skills and attitudes needed to thrive in an era of rapid
technological and socio-economic change. Mastery fosters confidence in problem-solving and provides a foundation
for understanding the world, reasoning logically, and appreciating the subject's value.

To achieve this, the syllabus mandates that teachers make mathematics relevant through practical, experiential
learning. Instruction should emphasize exploration and problem-solving, allowing pupils to conduct investigations
and build competence. The integration of ICT applications is also crucial to reflect technology's impact on modern

2.2 Competences

A competence is the ability to use an appropriate combination of knowledge, skills, attitudes, values, and behaviours
to accomplish a task successfully. In this syllabus, competences reflect a holistic blend of these elements, enabling
learners to apply them confidently and appropriately in diverse real-life situations.

By acquiring these competences, learners become better equipped to handle challenges, make informed decisions,
act responsibly, and contribute positively as active members of society. The competences are embedded in the
broad subject competences, yearly objectives, and unit-specific learning competences. Both generic competences
and basic competences must be emphasized and consistently reflected in the teaching and learning process.

Teachers are encouraged to design learning experiences that engage learners in meaningful tasks, allowing them
to practice and demonstrate competences both during and at the end of the learning process.

    2.2.1 Generic competences

Critical and problem-solving skills: Learners use different techniques to solve mathematical problems related to
real life situations. They are engaged in mathematical thinking, they construct, symbolize, apply and generalize ideas.

skills through mathematics contests, and mathematics competitions, etc.

3. Pedagogical Approach

The change to a competence-based curriculum is about transforming learning to ensure that learning is deep,
enjoyable and habit-forming.

IT in general and particularly ICT should be used as a pedagogical tool to facilitate teaching and learning of
mathematics. Various teaching strategies and approaches such as direct instruction, discovery learning,
investigation, guided discovery, guided practice or other methods must be incorporated. Among the approaches
that can be given consideration include the following:

   · Learner-centred learning;

   · Different learning abilities and styles of learners (individualization);

   · Use of relevant, suitable and effective teaching materials;

   · Formative evaluation to determine the effectiveness of teaching and learning processes.

The choice of a suitable approach will stimulate the teaching and learning environment inside or outside the
classroom. Suitable approaches include the following: Co-operative learning, Contextual learning, Mastery
learning and Constructivism.

3.1 Role of the Teacher

Teachers act as facilitators and guides, shifting away from traditional rote instruction. They organize learners
individually, in pairs, or in groups, and design purposeful tasks that promote inquiry, collaboration, and autonomy.
Teachers apply pedagogical principles of progression: moving from simple to complex, concrete to pictorial and
abstract, and from learners' experiences to broader knowledge rooted in Mathematic contexts. They integrate
cross-cutting issues such as peace, gender equality, environmental conservation, financial education, and cultural
values, while ensuring sensitivity to learners' diverse backgrounds. Teachers adopt an inclusive approach by
encouraging respect of ideas from colleagues.

3.2 Role of the Learner

In the competence-based curriculum, the learner is the principal actor of his/her education. He/she is not an
empty bottle to fill. Considering the initial capacities and abilities of the learner, the activities of the learner are
indicated against each learning unit and reflect appropriate engagement of the learner in the learning process. The
teaching-learning process will be tailored towards creating a learner friendly environment based on capabilities,
needs, experience and interests.

Therefore, the following are some of the roles of learners:

   · Learners construct the knowledge either individually or in groups in an active way. From the learning
       theory, learners move in their understanding from concrete through to pictorial to abstract. Therefore, the
       opportunities should be given to learners to manipulate concrete objects and to use models.

   · Learners will be encouraged to do research and present their findings through group work activities.

   · A learner is co-operative: learners work in heterogeneous groups to increase tolerance and understanding.

   · Learners are responsible for their own participation and for making sure others participate.

   · Help is sought from within the group and the teacher is asked for help only when the whole group agrees to
       ask a question.

   · Consensus on the answer is required from the whole group.

   · The group evaluates its own strategies and ideas rather than relying on the teacher for this evaluation.

   · The learners who learn at a faster pace do not do the task alone and then the others merely sign off on it.

   · Participants ensure the effective contribution of each member, through clear explanation and articulation of
       constructive arguments, to improve their English literacy, develop a sense of responsibility and to increase
       their self-confidence, and public speaking ability, etc.

3.3 Inclusive and Special Needs Education

In line with Rwanda's inclusive education policy, all learners regardless of ability have the right to access quality
education. Teachers adapt teaching methods, materials, and assessment strategies to meet diverse needs,
including those of learners with disabilities. Differentiated instruction, assistive technologies, peer support, and

4. Assessment Approach

Assessment within the CBC is a fundamental component of the teaching and learning process. It is designed
to evaluate learners' progress across knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values, while simultaneously supporting
continuous learning and development. Unlike traditional models, CBC assessment emphasizes real-life problem-
solving, requiring learners to apply acquired competences to complex, everyday situations.

Assessment entails the systematic collection and interpretation of evidence to measure learner performance
against established standards. Beyond measuring achievement, it informs instructional improvement and
provides constructive feedback to learners, parents, and educators, thereby fostering accountability and guiding
educational decision-making.

4.1 Levels of assessment across terms and national examinations

To provide a comprehensive picture of learner achievement and development, assessment is conducted at multiple
levels:

   i) Diagnostic: Conducted before instruction to identify learners' prior knowledge and skills; administered at
       the beginning of a unit or lesson to guide teaching.

   ii) Formative / Continuous: Carried out during the learning process to track progress and provide feedback;
       implemented continuously through quizzes, projects, class activities, and teacher observations.

10  MATHEMATICS SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6
   iii) Summative assessment:

       This is administered in form of comprehensive assessment under different authorities.

         ­ End Term I: Administered after instruction to evaluate learners' achievement of Term I content; conducted at the
           end of the first term as a school-based assessment.

         ­ End Term II: District-coordinated assessment designed to measure achievement of Term II content; developed by
           schools and quality-checked at district level.

         ­ End Term III: National assessment to evaluate mastery of the entire year's content; prepared, set, and moderated by
           NESA at the end of the third term.

         ­ National Examinations (NESA): High-stakes assessments for certification, selection, and accountability; administered
           nationally at the end of key education cycles (P6, S3, S6) under NESA's authority. This examination integrates
           objective questions, structured responses, and applied tasks aligned with CBC principles to holistically measure
           learner achievement.

Average scores from school, district, and comprehensive summative assessments will be weighted and included in
the final national examination grading, with the percentage gradually increasing as teachers gain experience and
confidence in assessment practices.

4.2 Record keeping

Accurate record keeping is essential for monitoring learner progress and informing instructional decisions.
Teachers use tools such as portfolios, checklists, and report books to collect evidence of learning. Portfolios include
samples of learners' work, test results, projects, and self-reflections, serving as both a record of achievement and
a diagnostic tool for guiding remedial action. These records provide data for feedback to learners, teachers, and
parents, and contribute to final evaluations. Results/marks for summative/comprehensive assessment will be
uploaded in Comprehensive Assessment Management Information System (CAMIS).

4.3 Item writing in summative assessment

Summative assessment items must be valid, fair, reliable, and aligned with CBC principles. They should test a
range of competences, including knowledge recall, application, analysis, and values integration, while reflecting
real-life contexts. Item writers follow a structured process that involves:

   · Identify topic areas to be tested from the subject syllabus.

   · Outline subject matter content to be considered as the basis for the test.

   · Identify learning outcomes to be measured by the test.

   · Preparing a test blueprint or table of specifications;

   · Using verbs that require higher-order thinking beyond memorization;

   · Applying transparent and consistent marking schemes.

   · Greater weight is given to higher-order cognitive skills.

4.4 Structure and format of national examinations

At the completion of primary education, learners undertake a national examination designed to evaluate literacy,
numeracy, and competences in core subjects. The paper for the exam will be comprised of two sections. The
first section will be composed of short answer items or items with short calculations which include questions
testing for knowledge and understanding, investigation of simple patterns, quick calculations and applications
of mathematics in real life through simple word problems. The second section will be composed of long answer
items or answers with constructions, simple demonstrations, investigation of simple patterns and generalization,
interpretation and explanations. The items for the second section will emphasize the mastering of mathematical
facts, the understanding of mathematical concepts and their application in real life situations. In this section, the
assessment will find out not only what skills and facts have been mastered, but also how well learners understand
the process of solving a mathematical problem and whether they can link the application of what they have
learned to the context or to the real life.

In this level, the following topic areas must be assessed: Numbers and operations, missing numbers, measurements

4.5 Reporting to parents

Regular communication with parents is an essential component of the CBC. This is achieved through progress
reports, parent­teacher meetings, and school community activities. Since the CBC emphasizes a wide range of
learning outcomes: knowledge, skills, attitudes, and competences--a single mark is insufficient to capture learner
progress. Effective reporting should highlight areas where learners are excelling as well as those requiring
improvement. Using descriptive feedback or simple rating scales (e.g., meeting expectations very well, meeting
expectations, not meeting expectations) across knowledge, subject-specific skills, and competences provides a
clearer and more comprehensive picture than numerical grades alone. This approach fosters meaningful parental
involvement and supports learners' continued growth.

5.4 Use of AI in Mathematics teaching and learning

AI became a powerful tool in mathematics education when used thoughtfully. The key is viewing AI as a tool in our
educational toolkit, not as a threat to genuine mathematical understanding.
Here is how teachers and students can ask a question to AI at different stages of the learning process.

MATHEMATICS SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6  17
1. Ways in which Mathematics teachers can use AI
 a) Lesson planning and content creation

· Generate practice activities: "Create 10 problems on quadratic equations with varying difficulty levels".

· Develop worksheets: "Make a worksheet on Pythagorean theorem with real-world applications".

· Create alternative explanations: "Explain complex numbers in 3 different ways for visual, auditory, and
    kinesthetic students".

· Generate assessment items: "Create a quiz on derivatives with answer key and scoring rubric".
 b) Differentiation and individualization

· Adapt problems: "Modify this calculus problem for a student who's struggling with algebraic manipulation".
· Create assignments: "Provide this geometry proof at basic, proficient, and advanced levels".
· Generate enrichment: "Create challenging problems about geometric sequences for advanced students".
 c) Assessment and feedback

· Analyze common errors: "Identify common misconceptions in solving linear equations and suggest addressing
    strategies".

· Generate rubrics: "Create a grading rubric for the activities on quantifiers and their negation".

· Provide feedback templates: "Suggest specific, constructive feedback for a student who made a sign error
    in solving inequalities".

 d) Professional development

· Stay current: "What are recent research-based strategies for teaching fractions?"

· Plan interventions: "Suggest interventions for students struggling with word problems".
· Designing projects to be assigned to students: "Help me design a project-based learning unit on exponential

    growth".

18  MATHEMATICS SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6
   2. Ways in which Mathematics students can use AI
    a) Personalized tutoring and explanation

   · Get multiple explanations: "Explain the chain rule in calculus using simple expressions to be understood by
       a S4 student".

   · Clarify concepts: "Why do we `invert and multiply' when dividing fractions? Explain visually".

   · Step-by-step guidance: "Walk me through solving this simultaneous equations step by step using matrices,
       but don't give me the answer".

    b) Practice and problem-solving

   · Generate similar problems: "Give me more problems like this one about probability".

   · Check understanding: "Create a self-check quiz on trigonometric identities".

   · Explore variations: "What if this geometry problem had different given information?"
    c) Conceptual deepening

   · Make connections: "How is the concept of slope related to real-world applications?"

   · Explore why: "Why does the quadratic formula work? Derive it for me".

   · Historical context: "What's the history behind the development of calculus?"

   3. Best Practices and guidelines

For Teachers, use AI as a Co-planner, not replacement. Verify and edit AI-generated content for accuracy. Teach
students HOW to use AI responsibly. Create AI-use policies for your classroom so that they focus on process over
answers.
For Students, use AI as a tutor, not an answer key. Show your work and ask for feedback on process. Ask "why"
and "how" not just "what's the answer". Do not copy AI solutions directly but use AI to understand methods. Do
not depend solely on AI but use AI to supplement learning. Do not trust AI blindly but verify answers and methods.

MATHEMATICS SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6  19
In conclusion, the most effective mathematics classrooms will be those where teachers use AI to enhance
their expertise and reach more students. Students use AI to get immediate, personalized support. Human
connection between teachers and students remains central for motivation and deep understanding. This means
that AI won't replace mathematics teachers, but teachers who use AI effectively will replace those who don't.

6. SYLLABUS UNITS

6.1 Presentation of the structure of the syllabus

The Mathematics syllabus is designed as a single, integrated syllabus implemented uniformly across all grades.
Its structure follows a logical progression that ensures clarity, consistency, and alignment with the Competence-
Based Curriculum approach.

At each grade level, the syllabus is organized into Topic Areas, which are further broken down into Sub-Topic
Areas. Each Sub-Topic Area is composed of Units, and every unit is characterized by the following features:

1. Alignment with Lessons: Each unit corresponds to a specified number of lessons, ensuring effective time
    allocation and instructional planning.

2. Key Unit Competence: Every unit is built around a Key Unit Competence that guides all teaching and learning
    activities. This competence represents the overall learning goal to be achieved by both teacher and learners.

3. Learning Objectives: Each key unit competence is translated into specific learning objectives, categorized as
    follows:

   · Type I (Knowledge and Understanding): Focused on foundational knowledge and comprehension, often
       referred to as Lower Order Thinking Skills (LOTS).

   · Type II and Type III (Skills, Attitudes, and Values): Aimed at developing practical skills, critical thinking,
       ethical values, and positive attitudes. These objectives, known as Higher Order Thinking Skills (HOTS), form
       the core focus of the adapted Competence-Based Curriculum.

20  MATHEMATICS SYLLABUS FOR UPPER PRIMARY | Primary 4 - 6
4. Content Specification: Each unit outlines the scope of content to be taught and learned, ensuring that teachers
    and learners work within the boundaries set by the stated learning objectives.

5. Learning activities: Each Unit suggests a non-exhaustive list of learning activities that are expected to engage
    learners in an interactive learning process as much as possible (learner-centred and participatory approach).

6. Link to other subjects: Finally, each Unit is linked to other subjects, the assessment criteria and the materials
    (or resources) that are expected to be used in the teaching and learning process.

The mathematics syllabus for upper primary level has got 37 units: 17 units in P4, 15 units in P5 and 15 units in P6.

6.3 Mathematics for primary five

    6.3.1 Key competencies at the end of primary five

At the end of primary five, learners can:
   · Read, write, compare and calculate whole numbers up to 1,000,000, add and subtract integers.
   · Solve problems involving measurements of time, length, capacity, mass, and money including calculating the
       number of intervals.
   · Solve simple problems involving proportions, fractions, and decimals.
   · Calculate circumference of a circle and volume of cuboids and cubes; draw and construct different angles.
   · Collect data, represent it, and interpret the results to answer a question or explore a hypothesis.
   · Solve missing number problems involving addition and subtraction.
   · Explain the concept of probability by conducting experiments to decide how likely something is to happen.

   · Read, write, compare, and calculate whole numbers beyond 1,000,000, multiply and divide integers, and use
       powers and indices, as well as the LCM and GCF when solving problems.
   · Convert between units of volume, capacity and mass, calculate simple interest, and solve problems involving
       savings.
   · Calculate speed, distance, and time.
   · Solve simple problems involving ratios, percentages, fractions, and decimals.
   · Calculate area enclosed by a circle, surface area of cuboids, and the volume of a solid.
   · Extend methods for collecting data, representing it, and interpreting it to answer a question.
   · Order events in terms of likelihood (impossible, equally likely, certain).
   · Use bearings and compass points, and understand the relationship between them. use the angle sum of a
       triangle to determine the interior angles of regular polygons.
   · Construct polygons using a protractor, a ruler, and a pair of compasses, and design nets to make cuboids
       and prisms.
